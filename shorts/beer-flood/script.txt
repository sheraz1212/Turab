In London, a street once drowned. In beer.
It's October, eighteen fourteen. At the Horse Shoe Brewery, giant wooden vats tower over the workers. The biggest is twenty-two feet tall, packed with dark porter.
Iron hoops hold each vat together, like belts on a very fat barrel.
One afternoon, a clerk notices a hoop has slipped. Just a few inches. His boss shrugs. "They slip all the time."
An hour later, the hoop snaps.
The vat bursts open. The wave smashes the next vat, and that one gives way too.
Hundreds of thousands of gallons roar straight through the back wall.
The beer floods the poor streets of St Giles, pouring into cellars where whole families live.
Rescuers wade in up to their waists, digging through the rubble.
Eight people lose their lives that day.
So who pays?
The court looks at the brewery, the broken hoop, the shrugging boss, and calls it an act of God.
Nobody is to blame.
And the brewery?
It asks the government for the tax it paid on all that lost beer.
And gets it back.
